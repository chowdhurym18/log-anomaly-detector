## The mechanism — distribution of `max_surprisal`, split by true label

If a regime truly learns *normal* behaviour, anomalous blocks should carry a **higher** surprise score than normal ones. The gap below is that effect.

| Regime | label | mean | median | p90 | p99 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| mixed | normal  | 0.7121 | 0.6562 | 0.9498 | 0.9975 |
| mixed | anomaly | 0.8279 | 0.9490 | 0.9976 | 0.9999 |

## Interpretation — the same model, the only change is the training data

**On DeepLog's own rule (`max_surprisal` — the single most surprising transition in a block), the effect is dramatic:** detection goes from F1 **0.212** / PR-AUC **0.125** (mixed) to F1 **0.721** / PR-AUC **0.654** (normal-only) — ΔF1 +0.509, ΔPR-AUC +0.528. Architecture, windows, loss and seed are identical; only the training data changed.

**The clearest mechanistic proof is the top-k-miss signal** (DeepLog's flag: was the actual event outside the model's top-3 predictions?). Under MIXED training it is **near-random (AUROC 0.581)** — anomalous blocks contain no more out-of-top-k events than normal ones, because the model was *taught* those transitions as ordinary. Under NORMAL-ONLY training it becomes **informative (AUROC 0.770)**.

**Why `mean_surprisal` moves little** (ΔF1 +0.020): averaging surprise over the whole block dilutes the one anomalous spike among many ordinary transitions. This is itself a finding — *peak / burst* aggregations beat *mean* for detection (see Task 2, `outputs/detection_analysis.md`).
